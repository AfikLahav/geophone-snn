# Acoustic Detection and Localization of Drones (UAVs) Using Distributed / Networked Microphone Arrays

*A technical literature review for a multi-sensor unattended-ground-sensor (UGS) node*

**Date:** 2026-06-25
**Context:** This report supports the extension of a buried-geophone seismic intrusion sensor (4.5 Hz geophone + edge ML classifier for human/vehicle/animal/nothing) into a multi-modal UGS node. A geophone is structurally blind to aerial threats; passive acoustics is the cheapest, lowest-power modality that can plausibly cover that blind spot. This document surveys the physics, signal processing, machine learning, datasets, performance limits, and counter-UAS positioning of acoustic drone detection, and assesses what is realistic on a low-power edge node.

---

## Abstract

Small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), especially multi-rotor quadcopters, radiate a distinctive tonal acoustic signature dominated by the blade-pass frequency (BPF) and its harmonics, typically with fundamental tones in the ~100–300 Hz band and a harmonic stack extending to roughly 10–20 kHz. This narrowband, harmonically structured emission is what separates a drone from broadband environmental noise and is exploited by both classical spectral methods and modern deep-learning classifiers, which reach 90–96 % detection accuracy on curated datasets (e.g., CNN at 96.4 % on the Al-Emadi DroneAudioDataset; DronePrint at 96 % closed-set / 86 % open-set). Networked and distributed microphone arrays add localization: time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA) via GCC-PHAT and beamforming (SRP, MVDR) yield bearing and, with multiple baselines, 3-D position with sub-degree to ~6° angular error and sub-meter to ~2 m positional error at favorable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR > 0–15 dB). The dominant constraint is range: practical detection of small quadcopters is ~50–500 m, collapsing in wind above ~5 m/s, in high ambient noise, and against deliberately quiet electric or fixed-wing platforms. Acoustics is therefore best understood not as a standalone counter-UAS sensor but as a passive, RF-silent, low-power, line-of-sight-independent cue that complements radar, RF, and EO/IR — and that fit, plus its modest compute footprint (Random Forest / small CNN on a Raspberry Pi-class or even MCU-class device), makes it the natural candidate to close a buried geophone's aerial blind spot at short range.

---

## 1. Introduction

A buried 4.5 Hz geophone couples to the ground and is sensitive to seismic surface waves generated by footsteps, vehicles, and digging. It has essentially zero sensitivity to airborne acoustic and aerial targets: a small quadcopter hovering or transiting overhead imparts negligible ground motion. As small UAVs become a primary threat vector for perimeter security and defense, a seismic-only node has a structural blind spot directly above and around it.

Passive acoustic sensing is attractive for closing this gap because it is (i) **passive** (no emission, undetectable by the target, no spectrum license), (ii) **cheap** (MEMS microphones cost cents to dollars), (iii) **low-power and low-compute** (classification runs on edge hardware), and (iv) **independent of line-of-sight** in the way radar and EO/IR are not — sound diffracts around foliage and structures. Its weaknesses (short range, wind/noise sensitivity, vulnerability to quiet platforms) are exactly the weaknesses that a layered multi-sensor node is meant to manage.

This report is organized around the six required topics: the acoustic signature (§2), distributed-array signal processing and localization (§3), machine learning and public datasets (§4), detection range and limiting factors (§5), the counter-UAS sensor-fusion picture (§6), and edge/UGS realism (§7).

## 2. The Acoustic Signature of Drones

### 2.1 Origin of the sound

The dominant acoustic emission of a multi-rotor drone is **tonal** and is produced at the propeller **blade-pass frequency (BPF)** and its integer harmonics. The BPF is set by rotor speed and blade count:

> **f_BPF = N_b × (RPM / 60)**

where N_b is the number of blades per rotor and RPM is rotor rotational speed. For a representative DJI Mavic-class quadcopter hovering at ~5000 RPM with 2-bladed props, the fundamental BPF is **≈166 Hz**. ([Drone Warfare – Acoustic Detection](https://drone-warfare.com/counter-uas/acoustic-detection/); [Acentech](https://www.acentech.com/resources/drone-noise-a-new-challenge-in-acoustics/))

### 2.2 Frequency content

- **Fundamental BPF tones:** ~**100–300 Hz** for most small drones; commercial-drone fundamentals often quoted as **150–400 Hz**. ([EAA/Forum Acusticum 2025 – Acoustic Detection of Drones](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2025/data/articles/000072.pdf); [Acentech](https://www.acentech.com/resources/drone-noise-a-new-challenge-in-acoustics/))
- **Harmonic stack:** a comb of tones at integer multiples of the BPF. Below ~2 kHz these appear as sharp narrowband peaks; harmonic energy extends to **~16 kHz**, with some platforms producing components up to **35–45 kHz** (ultrasonic). Most energy is concentrated in the low-kHz region; the practically useful band is commonly taken as **~100 Hz – 10 kHz**. ([Acentech](https://www.acentech.com/resources/drone-noise-a-new-challenge-in-acoustics/); [Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
- **Broadband component:** in addition to tones, turbulent/aerodynamic and motor noise contribute a broadband "whoosh" floor.

### 2.3 Why it is detectable / how detection exploits it

The diagnostic feature is the **harmonically structured, narrowband tonal stack** sitting above a broadband background. Natural and most man-made background noise (wind, traffic rumble, foliage) is broadband and lacks this regular harmonic comb, so detectors key on:

- **Spectral peaks at BPF and harmonics** (the "acoustic fingerprint"), often with spacing that identifies blade count and rotor speed.
- **Harmonic-to-noise structure** and the relative amplitudes of the harmonic series.
- **Micro-modulation:** to maneuver, a multi-rotor continuously trims individual rotor speeds, producing pitch/roll-correlated frequency deviations of the harmonics on the order of **20–30 %**, a dynamic cue absent in static tonal sources. ([Acentech](https://www.acentech.com/resources/drone-noise-a-new-challenge-in-acoustics/))

These cues are strong enough that some work claims **model-level identification** (not just "drone vs not-drone") from acoustics alone, because BPF, harmonic spacing, and broadband texture differ between platforms (see DronePrint, §4). ([EAA/Forum Acusticum 2025](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2025/data/articles/000072.pdf))

### 2.4 Practical features extracted

Classifiers typically operate on **log-mel spectrograms**, **MFCCs** (e.g., DronePrint uses 26 MFCCs), or raw short-time spectra, computed over short frames (tens to hundreds of ms), then fed to a classical ML or deep model (§4).

## 3. Distributed / Networked Microphone-Array Methods

Detection answers "is there a drone?"; **localization** answers "where?" Localization with microphones is fundamentally a spatial-sampling problem governed by wavelength.

### 3.1 The wavelength / spacing constraint

For unambiguous spatial processing the **spatial Nyquist (half-wavelength) criterion** must hold: inter-element spacing **d ≤ λ/2** at the highest frequency of interest, otherwise **grating lobes / spatial aliasing** appear and the direction-of-arrival becomes ambiguous. ([IEEE – On Spatial Aliasing in Microphone Arrays](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/4696048/); [Silicon Source – Beamforming Fundamentals](https://sistc.com/blog-microphone-beamforming-fundamentals/))

A worked example from a deployed MEMS array: an **8-mic square array with 15 cm spacing** is spatially unambiguous up to **≈1.1 kHz** (λ/2 = 15 cm ⇒ f ≈ 343/(2·0.15) ≈ 1.14 kHz). ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html)) Because the drone's strongest tones (BPF + first harmonics) lie below ~1 kHz, this spacing is well matched — wider spacing improves low-frequency directionality (larger aperture, finer bearing) but worsens aliasing at the higher harmonics; narrower spacing pushes the alias-free band up but shrinks aperture and angular resolution. This trade-off (aperture vs aliasing) is the central array-geometry decision. ([ResearchGate – On Spatial Aliasing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224355184_On_Spatial_Aliasing_in_Microphone_Arrays))

### 3.2 Core localization techniques

- **TDOA + GCC-PHAT:** estimate the relative arrival-time of the drone signal at mic pairs via the Generalized Cross-Correlation with Phase Transform, then intersect the resulting hyperboloids to get position/bearing. GCC-PHAT is the workhorse because the phase transform whitens the cross-spectrum and is robust to the colored, reverberant nature of outdoor sound. ([MDPI Sensors 25(6):1928](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/6/1928); [PMC11946234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11946234/))
- **Beamforming / Steered-Response Power (SRP-PHAT):** scan a grid of candidate directions, summing the phase-aligned signal power; the peak gives DOA. Coherent summation across N mics also raises array SNR.
- **MVDR (Minimum Variance Distortionless Response):** adaptive beamformer that nulls interferers while preserving the look direction. ([ResearchGate – Real-Time Joint Detection & Localization of UAVs](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349466267_A_Real-Time_System_for_Joint_Acoustic_Detection_and_Localization_of_UAVs))
- **3-D geometries (tetrahedral / hemispherical):** break the planar up/down (elevation) ambiguity inherent in a flat array, enabling unambiguous azimuth **and** elevation — important for an aerial target that is overhead. ([MDPI Sensors 26(6):1778 – Tetrahedral array + DNN](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/26/6/1778))

### 3.3 Single array vs distributed network

- **Single compact array** → **bearing only** (azimuth, and elevation if 3-D). Range is not directly observable from one small array.
- **Distributed / networked arrays** → **bearing fusion = position.** Two or more spatially separated arrays triangulate the target; a wide baseline gives range. This also coherently raises total SNR ("with numerous microphone units cooperating in the sound field, the total SNR of the system can be significantly improved, enabling detection of weak sounds"). Distributed networks spanning hundreds of meters can reach **sub-degree** angular accuracy but require **GPS/PTP synchronization to nanosecond-level timing** to keep TDOA valid across nodes. ([MDPI Sensors 25(6):1928](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/6/1928); [PMC11946234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11946234/))

### 3.4 Reported localization performance (quantitative)

| System | Array configuration | Method | Localization accuracy | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MEMS 8-mic planar array, Raspberry Pi 4 | 8× INMP441 MEMS, square, 15 cm spacing, 48 kHz | TDOA + Random Forest detect | **median 1.2 m** positional error; **6.3° ± 2.4°** mean angular error; 90 % within 2 m; ~40 ms loc latency | Outdoor, up to ~50–60 m | ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html)) |
| Distributed dual-array | 2 arrays × 4 mics (8 total), **2 m baseline**, 1 m intra-array spacing | GCC-PHAT, separate azimuth/elevation | **Simulation:** 0.157 m mean error (vs 2.49 m baseline). **Field:** 0.77 m mean (vs 2.45 m). Up to **2.13 m improvement at SNR > 0 dB**; <0.3 m at SNR > 15 dB; ~0.16 m at SNR > 20 dB | Field range limited to ~10 m (low source intensity), altitude 4–10 m | ([MDPI Sensors 25(6):1928](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/6/1928); [PMC11946234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11946234/)) |

**Takeaways for geometry/number-of-mics:** practical systems use **4–8 mics per array**, spacing **15 cm to 1 m** (matched to the sub-kHz drone tones), and **2+ arrays / ≥2 m baselines** for 3-D position. Localization quality degrades sharply below ~0 dB SNR and improves roughly monotonically (often near-exponentially) above it.

## 4. Machine-Learning Approaches and Public Datasets

### 4.1 Public datasets

| Dataset | Contents | Array / sensor | Notes | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DREGON** (DRone EGonoise and localizatiON, Inria) | In-flight quadrotor recordings, clean + noisy, continuously annotated with 3-D source position via motion capture; also per-rotor RPM and IMU | **8-channel mic array embedded on a quadrotor** (cube frame, two twisted parallel square layers) | Designed for UAV-**embedded** sound-source localization (search & rescue ego-noise problem); free for academic use | [dregon.inria.fr](https://dregon.inria.fr/); [IROS 2018 paper](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8593581/) |
| **DroneAudioDataset** (Al-Emadi et al.) | Audio of Parrot **Bebop** and **Mambo** flights; labels for detection (drone/not) and identification (Bebop/Mambo) | Single-channel collection | Widely used CNN/RNN/CRNN benchmark | [GitHub: saraalemadi/DroneAudioDataset](https://github.com/saraalemadi/DroneAudioDataset); [paper PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332727775_Audio_Based_Drone_Detection_and_Identification_using_Deep_Learning) |
| **DronePrint** corpus | Acoustic signatures of **13** commercial/consumer drone models, incl. online-sourced audio | — | Open-set detection + make/model ID | [IMWUT 2021 PDF](https://www.suranga.me/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/DronePrint_IMWUT.pdf) |
| **DroneAudioset** (search & rescue) | Large audio set for drone-based SAR (hearing **ground** sounds through ego-noise) | Multi-channel on-drone | Recent (2025) | [arXiv:2510.15383](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15383v1) |

*Note on DREGON scope:* DREGON's task is the inverse of perimeter detection — it localizes a ground source **from** a flying array while suppressing the drone's own ego-noise. It is nonetheless the canonical multi-channel drone-acoustics dataset and is heavily reused for array methods and ego-noise modeling.

### 4.2 Methods and reported accuracy

- **CNN / RNN / CRNN (Al-Emadi et al.):** CNN best on both tasks — **96.38 %** accuracy / 95.90 % F1 for **detection**, and **92.94 %** accuracy / 92.63 % F1 for **identification** (Bebop vs Mambo). ([paper](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Audio-Based-Drone-Detection-and-Identification-Deep-Al-Emadi-Al-Ali/75143f917642de78cd71d4216dd27db98b6ac7c4))
- **DronePrint (LSTM on 26 MFCCs, 13 models):** **96 %** closed-set detection, **86 %** open-set detection; cascaded make→model identification **90 %** overall; robust to hardware mods, Doppler, and varying SNR. ([IMWUT 2021 PDF](https://www.suranga.me/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/DronePrint_IMWUT.pdf))
- **Classical ML on MEMS array (Random Forest):** **91.6 %** average detection accuracy, precision 92–94 %, recall 85–89 %, on an 8-mic edge system — notable because it runs in real time on a Raspberry Pi 4. ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
- **Spectrogram vs raw time-series:** at a matched mid SNR, a spectrogram-input CNN scored **82.9 %** vs **50.1 %** for a time-series-image model — strong evidence that **time-frequency representations are the right input** for this problem. ([MDPI Drones 9(6):389](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/6/389))
- **Data augmentation:** GANs and SNR-mixing are used to expand limited drone corpora and harden classifiers against noise. ([MDPI Sensors 21(15):4953](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/21/15/4953))

**Caveat (publication-rigor note):** the headline 90–96 % figures are on **curated datasets**, often with limited drone diversity (sometimes two models) and favorable SNR. Real-world open-set, in-noise performance is materially lower (the open-set drop to 86 % in DronePrint, and the steep SNR dependence below, are the honest signal). Treat the high numbers as ceilings, not field expectations.

## 5. Detection Range and Limiting Factors

### 5.1 Typical ranges

- **Small quadcopters:** practical detection **~50–500 m**; quiet quadcopters often only **~200–300 m**; some compact MEMS edge systems demonstrate only **~50–60 m** outdoors (≈30 m indoors). ([Drone Warfare](https://drone-warfare.com/counter-uas/acoustic-detection/); [Airsight – Sensor Modalities](https://www.airsight.com/blog/drone-detection-technology-sensor-modalities); [Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
- **Loud fixed-wing / large platforms:** can be heard at **several kilometers** because they radiate far more acoustic power. ([Airsight](https://www.airsight.com/blog/drone-detection-technology-sensor-modalities))

Range scales with the target's radiated acoustic power and with SNR at the array; it is not a fixed number but a function of platform, environment, and array gain.

### 5.2 Limiting factors

1. **Geometric spreading (inverse-square law):** free-field SPL falls ~6 dB per doubling of distance — the hard physics ceiling on range.
2. **Atmospheric absorption (frequency-dependent):** high-frequency harmonics are absorbed faster than low-frequency tones, so at long range the signature is reduced to its lowest few harmonics — narrowing the usable feature set. ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
3. **Wind:** the single worst environmental factor — **severe degradation above ~5 m/s**, via both turbulent self-noise at the microphone and acoustic refraction/scattering of the propagating signal. ([Drone Warfare](https://drone-warfare.com/counter-uas/acoustic-detection/); [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/01/21/army-counter-drone-small-uas-acoustic-detection-systems/))
4. **Ambient noise (low SNR):** airports, highways, stadiums, and urban areas mask the signature; classification accuracy **rises with SNR, roughly exponentially**, and drops sharply as noise is added. Array cooperation (more mics, distributed nodes) is the main lever to recover SNR. ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html); [PMC11946234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11946234/))
5. **Quiet / electric / fixed-wing platforms:** the emerging hard case. Deliberately quiet electric UAVs produce almost no exploitable tonal energy — e.g., a quiet platform reported at only **14.9 dB at 1 km**, essentially inaudible against any realistic ambient floor; gliding fixed-wings can cut motor power entirely. ([DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/01/21/army-counter-drone-small-uas-acoustic-detection-systems/)) Manufacturers are actively designing quieter motors/props, eroding the acoustic signature over time. ([Drone Warfare](https://drone-warfare.com/counter-uas/acoustic-detection/))

### 5.3 SNR summary

Across studies the pattern is consistent: usable detection/localization needs roughly **SNR ≥ 0 dB**, becomes good around **≥ 15 dB**, and falls apart below **~−5 to 0 dB**. Distributed arrays buy back a few dB of effective SNR (coherent gain ≈ 10·log₁₀(N) for N mics in the ideal case), which is exactly why networking helps range and noise robustness.

## 6. Where Acoustic Fits in Counter-UAS (Sensor Comparison)

No single modality solves counter-UAS; operational systems **fuse** radar, RF, EO/IR, and acoustic. Each covers a different failure mode of the others. ([D-Fend](https://d-fendsolutions.com/anti-drone-detection/); [War Quants C-UAS Primer](https://www.warquants.com/p/the-war-quants-counter-uas-primer-c54))

| Modality | Detects via | Typical range | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Acoustic** | Radiated rotor/motor sound | ~50–500 m (small UAV) | Fully **passive** (target can't sense it), **RF-silent-immune** (works on autonomous drones), **no LOS needed** (sound diffracts around foliage/structures), no spectrum license, **cheap & low-power** | Short range; **wind- and noise-limited**; weak on quiet/electric/fixed-wing; bearing-only unless networked; coarse ID/position |
| **Radar** | RF echo (motion, RCS) | Hundreds of m – km | Long range, all-weather-ish, gives range+velocity, works on RF-silent drones | Needs **clear LOS**; small-UAV low-RCS hard; clutter (a hovering multirotor vs static infrastructure); active emission detectable |
| **RF / DF** | Drone↔controller comms | Often km+ | Long range, low cost, can ID make/model from signal library, gives operator bearing too | **Blind to autonomous / RF-silent** drones; congested spectrum; passive only if drone transmits |
| **EO / IR** | Visual / thermal imagery | LOS-limited (hundreds of m – km) | **Evidentiary-quality ID/imagery** (often required before mitigation), payload assessment | Needs clear **LOS + daylight/clear air**; defeated by fog/rain/darkness/foliage; field detection probabilities can be far below vendor claims (~40 % vs >90 %) |

**Acoustic's niche:** it is the modality that keeps working precisely where radar/RF/EO-IR struggle — **autonomous (RF-silent) drones, dense/forested/urban no-LOS environments, and covert passive operation** — and it is the cheapest to field densely. Its cost is short range and vulnerability to wind/noise and quiet platforms. Hence "acoustic as a short-range, always-on, passive **cueing** layer that hands off to longer-range/higher-fidelity sensors" is the consensus role. ([Drone Warfare – Acoustic](https://drone-warfare.com/counter-uas/acoustic-detection/); [Airsight](https://www.airsight.com/blog/drone-detection-technology-sensor-modalities); [arXiv:2507.19785 – Radar+Acoustic fusion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.19785))

## 7. Relevance to a Low-Power UGS Node (Covering the Geophone's Aerial Blind Spot)

### 7.1 Why acoustic, specifically, for this node

The geophone gives ground-threat coverage; the node needs a **short-range, passive, low-power** aerial cue, not a theater air-defense radar. Acoustic matches the UGS form factor and doctrine: passive (no emission to give away a hidden node), license-free, foliage-tolerant, and runnable on the same class of edge compute the geophone classifier already uses.

### 7.2 What is realistic on the edge (quantitative)

- **Sensors:** digital **MEMS microphones** (e.g., INMP441, ~62 dB SNR, 60 Hz–15 kHz, I²S) are adequate; cents-to-dollars each, tiny, low-power. An **8-mic, 15 cm square array** covers the sub-kHz drone tones without spatial aliasing. ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
- **Compute:** a full detect+localize pipeline (acquisition → mel/MFCC features → Random Forest classify → TDOA localize) runs in **real time on a Raspberry Pi 4 at 65–70 % CPU, <1 GB RAM, ~150 ms cycle latency**. Lighter detection-only inference is feasible on **Raspberry Pi 3B+** or an **ESP32-class MCU with DSP** at reduced frame rate. ([Acta Acustica 2026](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/full_html/2026/01/aacus250134/aacus250134.html))
- **TinyML path:** the broader TinyML acoustic literature (e.g., TinyChirp/TinyBird-ML bird-song nodes) shows small spectrogram CNNs running on ultra-low-power microcontrollers for always-on acoustic event detection — a credible template for a duty-cycled, battery/solar UGS. ([arXiv:2407.21453 – TinyChirp](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21453); [arXiv:2407.21486 – TinyBird-ML](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21486))

### 7.3 Recommended realistic design envelope

- **Role:** short-range (~50–300 m for typical loud multirotors; far less for quiet platforms) **passive cue + coarse bearing**, not a precision tracker.
- **Single-node output:** detection flag + class (drone vs non-drone, ideally vs the geophone's existing animal/vehicle/human/nothing taxonomy) + azimuth (and elevation with a 3-D/tetrahedral array).
- **Networked output:** if several UGS nodes are deployed, fuse per-node bearings across nodes (time-synced) to triangulate position — the distributed-array result (sub-meter to ~2 m at good SNR) is the achievable target, with the explicit caveat that it needs tight time sync and degrades hard in wind/noise.
- **Honest limits to design around:** expect collapse above ~5 m/s wind and in high ambient noise, and near-blindness to deliberately quiet electric/fixed-wing UAVs. Acoustic must be **layered**, never sole-sensor, for the aerial role.

### 7.4 Integration with the existing classifier

The acoustic front end mirrors the existing seismic pipeline (windowed time-frequency features → small edge classifier), so it can plug into the same edge-ML and event-reporting framework, extending the node's class set from {human, vehicle, animal, nothing} to additionally include **{drone}** on a parallel acoustic channel — directly filling the geophone's aerial blind spot at the node's immediate perimeter.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Multi-rotor drones broadcast a clear, harmonically structured acoustic signature (BPF fundamentals ~100–300 Hz plus a harmonic stack to ~10–20 kHz) that is readily distinguishable from broadband background and is exploited by classifiers reaching 90–96 % accuracy on curated data. Distributed/networked microphone arrays add localization through TDOA/GCC-PHAT and beamforming, achieving sub-degree to ~6° bearing and sub-meter to ~2 m position at favorable SNR, with array spacing dictated by the λ/2 spatial-Nyquist constraint (≈15 cm matching the sub-kHz drone tones). The binding limitation is range and robustness: ~50–500 m for small quadcopters, collapsing in wind (>5 m/s), in noise, and against quiet electric/fixed-wing platforms. Acoustic is therefore the wrong choice as a sole counter-UAS sensor but the **right choice as a passive, low-power, RF-silent, foliage-tolerant short-range cue** — exactly the profile needed to give a buried-geophone UGS node coverage of its aerial blind spot, runnable on the same edge-class hardware, provided it is deployed as one layer of a fused multi-sensor system rather than alone.
